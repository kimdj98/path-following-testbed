2 · Plant model 2/10 · Step 1

Three frames: ground, car, path

All three frames are right-handed with z pointing up. Angles and rotations are positive counter-clockwise (turning left).

Why write the equations of motion in the body frame?

  • Tyre forces are fixed to the car. In the body frame, Fyf and Fyr do not depend on the heading ψ. In the global frame every force picks up cos ψ, sin ψ and the equations become nonlinear in ψ.
  • Iz is constant. The axes rotate with the car, so the mass distribution does not change.
  • Sensors and the Simulink block give body-frame values. Vx, Vy and r come straight from an IMU or the Vehicle Body 3DOF block.
The price: because the frame rotates with the car, the acceleration picks up a rotation term (next slide).

The sign convention is the ISO 8855 vehicle frame: x forward, y left, z up. So δ > 0, r > 0 and ey > 0 all mean "left".

2 · Plant model 3/10 · Step 1

Equations of motion: Newton's laws in a frame that rotates with the car

Even if the components (Vx, Vy) stay the same, the vector itself rotates when the axes turn by r·dt. That change, ΔV ≈ (−rVy, rVx)·dt, is the rotation term added to the acceleration. Angles are exaggerated.

Acceleration in a rotating frame. Add a rotation term to the rate of change of the body-frame components.

\[ \mathbf a = \frac{d\mathbf V}{dt}\Big|_{\text{body}} + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf V,\qquad \boldsymbol\Omega = (0,\ 0,\ r),\ \ \mathbf V = (V_x,\ V_y,\ 0) \]
\[ a_x = \dot V_x - rV_y,\qquad a_y = \dot V_y + rV_x \]
Check: on a circle at constant speed, Vy and r are constant, so \(\dot V_y = 0\). Yet there is a centripetal acceleration ay = Vxr = Vx²/R, which the tyres must supply.

Forces and moment (bicycle model). The front tyre force acts lf ahead of the CG and turns the car counter-clockwise (+); the rear one acts lr behind it and turns it clockwise (−).

Fyf is normal to the wheel plane, so the front force, turned by δ, projects onto body y as \(F_{xf}\sin\delta + F_{yf}\cos\delta\). The front wheels are not driven or braked (Fxf = 0), and \(-F_{yf}\sin\delta\) lies along body x, in the dropped ax equation.

\[ m(\dot V_y + V_x r) = F_{yf}\cos\delta + F_{yr},\qquad I_z\,\dot r = l_f F_{yf}\cos\delta - l_r F_{yr} \]
  • Vx constant → drop the ax equation
  • δ ≤ 1.7° → cos δ ≈ 1 (error 0.05 %)
  • Planar motion → states left: Vy, r
Use the figure to explain the Ω×V term. Even if the components stay the same, the vector rotates when the axes rotate, and that change is rV_x. The circular-motion example (V̇_y = 0, yet there is a centripetal acceleration V²/R) makes it click. Explain the moment signs as "push the front wheel left and the car turns left; push the rear wheel left and it turns right". Holding V_x constant matches the Simulink block setting that takes V_x as an external input. If someone asks "why is there no sin δ in the y equation?", answer that sin δ multiplies the front longitudinal force F_xf, which is 0, and that F_yf sin δ goes into the x equation. When linearizing, cos δ = 1 − δ²/2, so it never survives in the first-order terms anyway.
2 · Plant model 4/10 · Step 2

Tyre force comes from the gap between where the wheel points and where it actually moves

The front axle moves with the combined velocity vf of Vx and Vy + lfr, but the wheel is turned by δ. The difference is αf. Each lateral force is perpendicular to its wheel plane. Angles are exaggerated for clarity.

Front tyre force Fyf [kN] vs slip angle α
Linear C·αSaturating model

Bicycle model. Merge the left and right wheels into one in the middle. Only the front wheel steers. When the car rotates at r, the front axle moves lfr further to the left and the rear axle lrr further to the right.

Slip angle. The difference between where the wheel points and where the wheel centre actually moves. For small angles,

\[ \alpha_f = \delta - \frac{V_y + l_f r}{V_x},\qquad \alpha_r = -\,\frac{V_y - l_r r}{V_x} \]

The tyre produces a lateral force proportional to this angle. Think of it as twisted rubber springing back.

\[ F_{yf} = C_f\,\alpha_f,\qquad F_{yr} = C_r\,\alpha_r \]

The chart uses a saturating (Dugoff) model with a static load of 8.4 kN and a friction coefficient of 0.9. The baseline's slip angles stay below 1.7°, so the linear approximation holds.

Slip angle may be new to students, so explain it with the figure. The angle between the dashed line (where the wheel points) and v_f (where it actually moves) is α_f. The tyre is a spring that produces a force proportional to that angle. The chart is the evidence that we stay inside the linear range. Likely questions: (1) "Can the car not steer without slip?" The only force that pushes the car sideways comes from the tyres, and F = Cα, so α = 0 means zero force. The moment you steer, α_f = δ appears, and that is how steering turns the car. At low speed the required force scales with V² and is tiny, so α ≈ 0 is a fine approximation (the kinematic model). (2) "Is it friction?" Most of the contact patch sticks to the road, so it is static friction. The force is set by the rubber deformation Cα; μF_z (about 7.5 kN on the front axle) is only the upper limit. Where the chart bends is where the rear of the contact patch starts to slide. (3) "Is it a restoring force?" From the rubber's side it is the elastic restoring force of the deformed rubber, and it acts to reduce α. It is not the self-aligning torque that returns the steering wheel to centre.

Put the tyre forces into the equations of motion and you get a familiar second-order system

Step 1 equation of motion (cos δ ≈ 1): lateral
\[ m(\dot V_y + V_x r) = F_{yf} + F_{yr} \]
Yaw
\[ I_z\,\dot r = l_f F_{yf} - l_r F_{yr} \]

Substitute Fyf and Fyr from step 2 and collect the terms in Vy, r and δ:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix} V_y \\ r \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -\dfrac{C_f + C_r}{mV_x} & \dfrac{-C_f l_f + C_r l_r}{mV_x} - V_x \\[2mm] \dfrac{-C_f l_f + C_r l_r}{I_z V_x} & -\dfrac{C_f l_f^2 + C_r l_r^2}{I_z V_x} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} V_y \\ r \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{C_f}{m} \\[2mm] \dfrac{C_f l_f}{I_z} \end{bmatrix}\delta \]
\[ \frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix} V_y \\ r \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3.125 & -19.69 \\ 0.25 & -5.65 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} V_y \\ r \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 31.25 \\ 35 \end{bmatrix}\delta \]

2 · Plant model 7/10 · Step 4

Measure the state relative to the path and the goal becomes x = 0

ey: distance from the closest point P to the CG, measured along the normal (left +). eψ = ψ − ψp: difference between the vehicle heading and the path tangent.

Kinematics. If the forward velocity Vx is tilted by eψ relative to the path, the car moves away from the path at Vxeψ, and the lateral velocity Vy adds to that. The car must also turn as fast as the path does (\(\dot\psi_{des}\)) for the heading error to stay at zero.

\[ \dot e_y = V_y + V_x\,e_\psi,\qquad \dot e_\psi = r - \dot\psi_{des},\qquad \dot\psi_{des} = V_x\kappa \]

Substitution. Put the inverted Vy, r into the step 3 equations and use the derivative of the relations above. The next slide walks through the substitution.

\[ V_y = \dot e_y - V_x e_\psi,\qquad r = \dot e_\psi + \dot\psi_{des} \]
\[ \ddot e_y = \dot V_y + V_x\,\dot e_\psi,\qquad \ddot e_\psi \approx \dot r \quad (\ddot\psi_{des} \approx 0) \]
The vehicle dynamics stay the same; only the reference for the state changes. As a result, "follow the path" becomes "drive x to 0 (regulation)", so δ = −Kx can be used directly. The \(\dot\psi_{des}\) hidden inside r comes out as the disturbance Bd.
This step is a change of coordinates, not new physics. Explain ė_y = V_y + V_x e_ψ with the figure: a car moving forward at an angle drifts away from the path. The full derivation is in plant_model.html Step 4. If someone asks "can't the closest point suddenly jump?", answer that as long as the car is closer to the path than the radius of curvature R (1 − κe_y > 0), the closest point is unique and moves continuously. This path has R ≥ 1041 m and |e_y| ≤ 7.7 cm, so κe_y is at most 4×10⁻⁵. For paths that come back near themselves, such as a U-turn, search only near the previous point.
2 · Plant model 8/10 · Step 4 in detail

Rewrite the vehicle-state [Vy, r] equations in the error state x

The step 3 two-state model, written with named coefficients.

\[ \begin{aligned} \dot V_y &= \class{mA}{a_{11}}\,V_y + \class{mA}{a_{12}}\,r + \class{mB}{b_1}\,\delta \\ \dot r &= \class{mA}{a_{21}}\,V_y + \class{mA}{a_{22}}\,r + \class{mB}{b_2}\,\delta \end{aligned} \]

a11 = −3.125, a12 = −19.69, a21 = 0.25, a22 = −5.65, b1 = 31.25, b2 = 35

The step 4 substitution turns the vehicle states into errors.

\[ V_y = \dot e_y - V_x e_\psi,\qquad r = \dot e_\psi + \dot\psi_{des} \]
\[ \ddot e_y = \dot V_y + V_x\,\dot e_\psi,\qquad \ddot e_\psi \approx \dot r \]

Substituting removes the vehicle states and leaves only the errors and their rates.

\[ \begin{aligned} \ddot e_y &= \class{mA}{a_{11}}\,\dot e_y + \class{mA}{(-V_x a_{11})}\,e_\psi + \class{mA}{(a_{12} + V_x)}\,\dot e_\psi + \class{mB}{b_1}\,\delta + \class{mBd}{a_{12}}\,\dot\psi_{des} \\ \ddot e_\psi &= \class{mA}{a_{21}}\,\dot e_y + \class{mA}{(-V_x a_{21})}\,e_\psi + \class{mA}{a_{22}}\,\dot e_\psi + \class{mB}{b_2}\,\delta + \class{mBd}{a_{22}}\,\dot\psi_{des} \end{aligned} \]

Add the two definitional integrals \(\tfrac{d}{dt}e_y = \dot e_y\) and \(\tfrac{d}{dt}e_\psi = \dot e_\psi\) and write everything as matrices to get A, B, Bd.

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix} e_y \\ \dot e_y \\ e_\psi \\ \dot e_\psi \end{bmatrix} = \class{mA}{\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & a_{11} & -V_x a_{11} & a_{12} + V_x \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & a_{21} & -V_x a_{21} & a_{22} \end{bmatrix}} \begin{bmatrix} e_y \\ \dot e_y \\ e_\psi \\ \dot e_\psi \end{bmatrix} + \class{mB}{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ b_1 \\ 0 \\ b_2 \end{bmatrix}}\delta + \class{mBd}{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ a_{12} \\ 0 \\ a_{22} \end{bmatrix}}\dot\psi_{des} \]
  • Rows 1 and 3 are the definitional integrals; rows 2 and 4 are the step 3 vehicle dynamics as they are.
  • The eψ column −Vxa11 = 62.5, −Vxa21 = −5 comes from Vy = \(\dot e_y\) − Vxeψ. If the car is turned relative to the path, the same \(\dot e_y\) means a different actual sideslip.
  • A24 = a12 + Vx = −19.69 + 20 = 0.3125. The −Vx inside a12 and the +Vx\(\dot e_\psi\) in \(\ddot e_y\) almost cancel.
  • Bd is the r column (a12, a22) of the step 3 matrix. Since r = \(\dot e_\psi\) + \(\dot\psi_{des}\), the rate at which the path turns enters through the same channel as r.
Name the coefficients of the step 3 matrix a_ij and show that rows 2 and 4 of the 4-state A are filled with exactly those coefficients. Check one number: A23 = −20 × (−3.125) = 62.5. The key point of this slide is that B_d equals the r column of the step 3 matrix. The path's turn rate is the share of turning the car has to do, so it enters where r does.

2 · Plant model 10/10

With the numbers in, three control properties stand out

\[ \mA = \class{mA}{\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -3.125 & 62.5 & 0.3125 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0.25 & -5 & -5.65 \end{bmatrix}},\quad \mB = \class{mB}{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 31.25 \\ 0 \\ 35 \end{bmatrix}},\quad \mBd = \class{mBd}{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -19.69 \\ 0 \\ -5.65 \end{bmatrix}} \qquad (V_x = 20\ \mathrm{m/s}) \]
\[ \frac{e_y(s)}{\delta(s)} = \frac{31.25\,s^2 + 187.5\,s + 2343.75}{s^2\,(s^2 + 8.775\,s + 22.58)} \]

This is the transfer function with ey as the only output, \(C(sI - A)^{-1}B,\ C = [1\ 0\ 0\ 0]\). The signal passes steering → yaw rate (the step 3 second-order system) → heading (integrate) → ×Vx → lateral position (integrate), which puts s² in the denominator.

Open-loop eigenvalues 0, 0, −4.39 ± 1.82i

The s² in the denominator is the integration to position and heading. Without steering, the car does not return to the path (marginally stable). The complex pair is the vehicle yaw mode from step 3.

Controllability rank ctrb(A, B) = 4

One steering input can move all four states, so all four poles can be placed wherever we want.

Curvature disturbance Bd ≠ 0

When the path bends, an error appears regardless of steering. Feedback reacts only after the error appears, so on its own it leaves a small lag during the lane change (slide 16).

The design uses this linear model; verification uses the nonlinear Vehicle Body 3DOF block. For a 1° steering step, the two models differ by less than 0.3% (tests/run_tests.m check 5a).

From here on it is the language of the control course. Cover three points: the double integrator means feedback is required, controllability means pole placement is possible, and B_d means feedback alone has limits. The third point sets up the results on slide 16.
